\subsection{Overall Retrieval Effectiveness}
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarizes retrieval performance across all configurations and tiers. 
At the two-stage reranking tier, \lineH\ achieves the highest aggregate effectiveness ($\text{NDCG@10} = 0.621$, 95\% CI $[0.555, 0.682]$), alongside the strongest Top-1 accuracy ($\text{NDCG@1} = 0.800$) and reciprocal rank ($\text{MRR@10} = 0.950$). 
\lineN\ follows with $\text{NDCG@10} = 0.580$ ($[0.505, 0.649]$), while \astH\ achieves $\text{NDCG@10} = 0.487$ ($[0.419, 0.551]$) and \astN\ drops to $0.341$ ($[0.263, 0.421]$).

Lexical BM25 scores lower than neural tiers in all conditions (NDCG@10 between $0.305$ and $0.433$ across the four BM25 configurations in the package; Table~\ref{tab:main} lists all configurations). 
Because BM25 was evaluated alongside dense and rerank systems in our unified single-wave pool, Judged@3 and Judged@5 reach $100.0\%$ for all 12 systems, while Judged@10 ranges between $56.0\%$ and $75.2\%$. 
We also report bpref, which evaluates ranking quality solely among judged items~\cite{buckley2004bpref}. 
BM25 bpref ranges from $0.129$ to $0.255$, compared to the dense ($0.179$--$0.347$) and reranking tiers ($0.170$--$0.353$). 
With headers, dense retrieval improves NDCG@10 over BM25 by $0.118$ to $0.139$, and reranking adds a further $0.037$ to $0.070$. 
Notably, context headers benefit AST chunks already at the dense bi-encoder tier ($\Delta = +0.092$, rising from $0.358$ to $0.450$) as well as during reranking ($\Delta = +0.146$, reaching $0.487$), confirming that headers serve as essential anchors during initial candidate retrieval.

\textbf{RQ1: Context Header Impact (Comps 3 and 4).}
Adding hierarchical context headers produces a statistically significant improvement for AST chunks (\textbf{Comp 3}: $\Delta = +0.146$, 95\% CI $[+0.086, +0.207]$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 0.0001$, $W = 24.0$). 
\astH\ outperforms \astN\ on 23 of the 25 JDs. 
In contrast, prepending context headers to line windows yields a small gain that we cannot distinguish from zero (\textbf{Comp 4}: $\Delta = +0.041$, 95\% CI [$-0.012$, $+0.093$], $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 0.2411$, $W = 118.0$; 15 wins vs. 10 losses). 
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction_plot}, context headers act as an essential anchor for AST chunks while providing marginal benefits for line windows.

\textbf{RQ2: AST vs. Line-Window Chunking (Comps 1, 2, and 5).}
Comparing \astH\ against \lineN\ reveals that line windows without headers significantly outperform AST chunks with headers (\textbf{Comp 1}: $\Delta = -0.093$, 95\% CI [$-0.164$, $-0.023$], $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 0.0438$, $W = 78.0$), with Line winning 18 vs. AST winning 7. 
Without headers (\textbf{Comp 2}), line windows outperformed AST chunks ($\Delta = -0.239$, 95\% CI [$-0.292$, $-0.185$], $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < 0.001$, $W = 3.0$), winning 23 of 25 queries. 
In the exploratory contrast with headers (\textbf{Comp 5}), line windows remain significantly higher ($\Delta = -0.134$, 95\% CI [$-0.199$, $-0.070$], $p_{\mathrm{raw}} = 0.0003$, $W = 36.0$; Line wins 20, AST wins 5, 0 ties).

\subsection{Language Sensitivity and Fallback Audit}
Subgroup analysis across target ecosystems reveals consistent trends:
On Java repositories ($N=15$ JDs), \lineH\ outperforms \astH\ ($\text{NDCG@10} = 0.716$ vs. $0.583$, $\Delta = -0.133$). 
On React/TypeScript repositories ($N=10$ JDs), the pattern is identical ($\text{NDCG@10} = 0.478$ vs. $0.342$, $\Delta = -0.136$).

Following the grammar expansion in our Tree-sitter parser, modern constructs in both ecosystems (e.g., Java records, enums, annotated DTO classes; React arrow function components, hooks, interfaces, and type aliases) are captured as first-class syntactic nodes. 
As a result, the AST fallback rate across the entire corpus is $0.0\%$ ($0/438$ chunks), ensuring that syntactic nodes maintain $87.2\%$ boundary intactness without falling back to sliding windows. 
In the top-10 retrieved results for the AST arm, fallback chunks constitute $0.0\%$ ($0/250$) across all tiers.
