\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Possible Explanations}
Our results are consistent with recent controlled findings in code completion~\cite{wu2026chunking} and do not support the assumption that AST-first chunking is inherently better. 
They are also consistent with, but do not establish, two explanations. 
First, headers may supply lexical cues (file, class, and method names) that a cross-encoder can match against requirement terms; this could explain why headers help short AST chunks (mean 19.8 LOC) more than 50-line windows (mean 41.0 LOC), which already contain surrounding declarations and imports. 
Second, because 35.7\% of AST-arm chunks are line-window fallbacks (mean 42.9 LOC) that account for 46\% to 70\% of the top-10 AST results, the AST arm mixes two chunk types, so our comparison cannot separate syntactic boundaries from chunk size and content density. 
While formal parser refactoring is scheduled in our roadmap (Section~\ref{sec:plan}), our subgroup analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results}-C already shows that in Java (where fallback is only $13.1\%$), AST closely tracks line windows ($\Delta = -0.021$). 
We also note that the 100\% boundary-intact rate of pure AST chunks follows from how they are extracted and is not an empirical finding.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\textbf{Internal validity.} 
The AST arm contains 35.7\% line-window fallbacks, so it represents an AST-first hybrid strategy rather than pure AST; we provide a complete fallback audit and offline pure-AST pipeline scripts in the replication package. 
Annotator blinding applies only to the 353 pooled items; the gold items may have been labeled with header information visible, and we did not run a header-bias check. 
Agreement is lower on the pooled items than on the gold items, and the adjudication notes follow structured templates. 
Relevance judgments come from pooling the top-3 results of eight dense and reranking systems, so BM25 and deeper ranks are partly unjudged (Judged@10 is about 54\% to 59\% for dense and reranking, and 14\% to 30\% for BM25); unjudged items count as zero, so NDCG@10 is a lower bound. 
In addition, 78 of the 250 gold pairs refer to chunk boundaries from an earlier extraction and cannot be retrieved from the current corpus; restricting judgments to the 525 retrievable pairs leaves the comparisons essentially unchanged (C1 $\Delta = -0.015, p = 0.85$; C2 $\Delta = -0.129, p < 0.001$; C3 $\Delta = +0.114, p = 0.001$; C4 $\Delta = +0.029, p = 0.40$; C5 $\Delta = -0.044, p = 0.14$).

\textbf{External validity.} 
The study uses 25 JDs and 40 repositories in two ecosystems (Java Spring Boot and React/TypeScript), one embedding model, and one reranker; results may differ for other languages and models. 
With 25 queries, the smallest paired difference we could detect with 80\% power ranges from about 0.07 to 0.14 NDCG@10 across the five comparisons (paired $t$-test approximation, $\alpha = 0.05$ to $0.0125$); smaller differences are unlikely to be detected, so non-significant results mean absence of evidence, not equivalence.

\textbf{Construct validity.} 
Requirements are extracted by an LLM (\texttt{gemini-1.5-flash}) from public job postings, which may introduce wording artifacts, and relevance uses a three-level scale.

\textbf{Ethics and licensing.} 
Repositories are public; 19 use MIT, 4 Apache-2.0, 1 AGPL-3.0, and 16 state no license. 
The package includes chunk text for replication; copyright remains with the original authors. 
The study retrieves evidence for requirements and does not score individual developers.
